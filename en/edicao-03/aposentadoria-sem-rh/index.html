Who takes care of your retirement
when you don’t have
an HR department to handle it?

For professionals working through business entities, the self-employed, and independent practitioners, social security planning isn’t standard practice—but that doesn’t mean it can’t be tailored to your needs

By Fernanda Sodré

She closed her laptop after issuing the last invoice of the month. Fifteen years as a consultant, loyal clients, a full schedule. It wasn’t until a friend mentioned her Social Security statement that she realized: she had never stopped to check how much time she had actually contributed under her name. For years, she had assumed that issuing invoices and paying taxes took care of that part of life. It didn’t.

This scenario, with minor variations, plays out every day in the routine of those who serve corporate clients, self-employed individuals, and independent professionals. Unlike those who work under the CLT (Consolidated Labor Laws) regime—where social security contributions are made automatically, deducted from paychecks, and recorded without the worker having to think about it—for those who are self-employed, this responsibility isn’t built into any process. No one tells them. No one deducts the contributions. And, in the absence of an HR department to handle this, retirement becomes a project that must be actively managed—or it simply won’t happen.

The difference no one explains

The Brazilian social security model was structured around the employer-employee relationship: the company makes the payments, the INSS records them, and the worker passively monitors the process. For legal entities, the self-employed, and independent professionals, the logic is completely reversed. Contributions depend on the taxpayer’s choice of category, tax rate, and tax base—and each of these choices has a direct impact on the amount and timing of future benefits.

“The most common mistake isn’t failing to contribute. It’s contributing without knowing exactly what benefits you’re building up with those contributions.”

Add to this a factor that particularly affects those with variable income — common among self-employed professionals and businesses that depend on projects, contracts, or seasonal fluctuations: irregular contributions, varying tax rates over the years, and changes in tax classification that, if not monitored, create silent gaps in the contribution history. None of these gaps appear immediately. They usually only come to light when the professional finally decides to apply for benefits—by which time it is no longer possible to correct the past.

The myth that nothing can be done

One of the most common obstacles isn’t technical; it’s psychological: the belief that, because they aren’t formally employed, self-employed professionals are at the mercy of whatever is left over—with no real control over their own social security future. This belief is the main reason why so many people put off addressing the issue indefinitely.

“Planning isn’t about having a ready-made formula. It’s about looking at each professional’s reality—income, age, contribution category—and building a strategy that makes sense for that specific case.”  

In practice, there is now considerable room for customization: adjusting the taxpayer category according to the career stage, simulations that compare different contribution scenarios, specific strategies for those who have a registered business (CNPJ) but also provide services as individuals, and planning for those who intend to transition from self-employment to a corporate structure—or vice versa. Each of these situations requires a different approach, and this is precisely where the difference between “paying taxes” and “planning” becomes most apparent: the former is automatic; the latter requires an informed decision.

“Self-employed professionals usually have more freedom of choice than they realize. The problem is that this freedom only helps when it’s used with information; otherwise, it just becomes yet another postponed decision.”  

PJ, self-employed, independent professional: why the strategy isn’t the same

These three profiles are often treated as synonyms in everyday life, but each faces a different challenge when it comes to planning. Those who have a CNPJ (Corporate Taxpayer ID), even as a sole proprietor, generally pay social security contributions as individual taxpayers on their pro-labore income and must choose between a reduced contribution plan—which costs less but limits access to certain benefits—and a full plan, which guarantees full eligibility for retirement benefits based on contribution time. This choice, made without guidance, often follows the wrong criterion: what costs less each month, rather than what guarantees more in the long run.

On the other hand, self-employed individuals without a formal business structure—who provide services as individuals—often confuse the INSS withholding made by the hiring party with a full contribution—when, in practice, this withholding may not cover the amount needed to complete the contribution period for the month. And self-employed professionals who earn part of their income under the CLT (Consolidated Labor Laws), part through one-off services, and part through a business entity (PJ) must correctly combine the time and amounts from both systems—something that does not happen automatically and, if neglected, causes the professional to lose contribution time that was already rightfully theirs.

Three profiles, three different points of attention—and that is exactly why a one-size-fits-all approach rarely works. What works is examining each professional’s specific situation before recommending any course of action.

From Awareness to Action

This is the role AposentaSP plays: not to alarm those who should have already taken action, but to show that social security planning for those without a CLT employment contract can and should be handled with the same technical care given to any other strategic business decision. Simulations, reviewing contribution history, and adjusting employment categories are all part of a process that begins, in most cases, with a simple question: Do you know, right now, what you’re building for your future?

For those building a career outside the traditional employment structure, this question has no standard answer. There is, however, a possible answer—provided someone is willing to address it in time.
