Before negotiating high-value contracts or leading a company with operations in three countries, Emanuel Castilho taught math. Sixteen years later, at the helm of Casa Castilho and with more than 5,000 projects completed, he still defines leadership through the same verb: to teach.

By Fernanda Sodré
“Processes teach you what to do. A leader teaches you how to think.”

His career path did not follow a straight line. Teacher, engineer, fire protection specialist, business strategist, CEO—Emanuel connected fields that, at first glance, seemed worlds apart. His entrepreneurial journey began in a practical way: while in college, he worked maintaining and refilling fire extinguishers. What started as a side job became the starting point for a specialization that today underpins a company with operations in Brazil, the United States, and Portugal.

In the fire protection sector, a wrong technical decision can compromise much more than just compliance with a standard: it can put lives, assets, and the continuity of an operation at risk. For Emanuel Castilho, safety must be understood as part of any company’s strategy and risk management.

Throughout his tenure at the helm of Casa Castilho, he realized that technical excellence, while indispensable, does not guarantee growth on its own. Many companies are extremely competent in what they deliver, but they do not master the process of transforming that competence into perceived value, predictable sales, and profitability.

It was from this realization that the second pillar of his work was born. Emanuel began to study sales, positioning, and negotiation with the same discipline he applied to engineering, and today he has trained over a thousand professionals through lectures and training sessions in Brazil and abroad.

His business vision is based on a simple principle: selling is not just about making an offer. It is first and foremost about understanding the customer—their pain points, risks, and goals—and then presenting the appropriate solution.

“It’s not enough to be excellent at what you do. If your company doesn’t know how to turn competence into perceived value, predictable sales, and profit, it will continue to rely on chance to grow.”

“An objection isn’t a rejection. It’s a request for clarity and reassurance.”

In practice, this means replacing pressure with analysis. In high-value negotiations, the question that matters isn’t how much the customer is willing to invest, but what risk they need to eliminate.

This is also why he argues that price is something you compare—authority is built through a combination of technical knowledge, consistency, and delivery.

This logic also permeates the way he leads. For Emanuel, a scalable company isn’t one where the founder has all the answers, but one capable of developing people who make sound decisions even without him around—a principle that, according to him, is a direct legacy of the classroom.

Today, that classroom has taken on a new dimension: Emanuel chairs BNI Pontes in Bauru, a group that has grown to nearly 40 members in a short time and is already making a name for itself in the Central São Paulo region. For him, the group’s growth confirms something that also applies to business—a network isn’t measured by what you receive from it, but by the value you can generate for it before asking for anything in return.

The phrase that best captures his leadership style is “The Maximum at the Very Least.” More than just a saying, it represents a culture rooted in discipline, responsibility, and excellence.

Over the course of 16 years in business, the challenges have evolved: acquiring technical knowledge, building the company, structuring processes, forming teams, and conquering the market. Today, Emanuel understands that the next step is to further intensify the development of people capable of spreading this culture, taking on responsibilities, training new leaders, and sustaining the company’s growth with increasing autonomy.

“A leader’s true legacy is to build a culture that continues to generate excellence even when he is not present.”

The teacher hasn’t left the classroom. He’s just shifted to a larger scale.
